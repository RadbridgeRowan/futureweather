What is a future weather file, and when do you need one?

By Rowan Schmidt, Head of Product, FutureWeather · November 18, 2026

A future weather file is a year of hourly weather data, usually in the EnergyPlus EPW format, that represents the climate expected at a site in a future period, such as the years around 2050, rather than the recent past. You use it exactly like a normal weather file in EnergyPlus, OpenStudio, Ladybug, IES VE and similar tools. Reach for one when a decision depends on how a building or system will perform over its life: HVAC sizing, energy and cost projections, and overheating or resilience checks.

What's in a weather file?

An EPW file holds 8,760 hourly records, one for every hour of a year. Each record includes dry-bulb temperature, dew point, relative humidity, air pressure, global, direct and diffuse solar radiation, longwave (infrared) radiation, wind speed and direction, sky cover and precipitation. A header adds the location, ground temperatures and a summary of design conditions [1].

The weather files most people download, such as TMY3 or TMYx files, are typical years. Each one is assembled from the most representative months of a multi-year historical record [2, 3]. The Seattle-Tacoma TMYx file in the example below, for instance, is built from weather recorded between 2011 and 2025, and its header records which year each month came from: January from 2023, July from 2022, and so on.

Why isn't a historical weather file enough?

A typical year describes the climate of the period it was built from, and that climate is not standing still. The IPCC estimates that global surface temperature in 2011–2020 was already 1.09 °C higher than in 1850–1900, and projects that it will keep rising until at least the middle of the century under all the emissions scenarios it considered [4]. Older files, such as TMY3, are built from even earlier decades.

Buildings and the systems in them are designed now but operate for decades. If you size equipment or check comfort only against the past climate, you are testing the design against conditions it may rarely see later in its life. A future weather file lets you run the same simulation against the climate of the 2040s, 2050s or later and compare the results.

How is a future weather file made?

A common and well-documented way to make future weather files for building simulation is morphing: a real historical file is adjusted, hour by hour, by the change that global climate models project between a recent baseline period and a future period [5]. Because each hour is adjusted rather than invented, the future file keeps realistic day-to-day weather patterns. FutureWeather uses 23 CMIP6 climate models and the peer-reviewed Future Weather Generator method [6]. Our guide to how future weather files are made walks through each step.

When should you use one?

  • HVAC sizing and equipment selection. Check heating and cooling capacity against future design conditions, using a future DDY design-day file alongside the EPW.
  • Energy and cost over the building's life. Estimate how energy use and operating cost shift, for capital planning and operating budgets.
  • Overheating, thermal comfort and resilience. Test passive and mechanical strategies against warmer summers.
  • Comparing design options. Run the same alternatives against several future years and scenarios to see which ones hold up.
  • Renewables and data centers. Model PV output with future irradiance and temperatures, or size cooling for future wet-bulb conditions.

Keep the historical file wherever a code compliance path or rating system prescribes the weather data to use. Future-climate results then sit alongside the compliance model rather than replacing it.

What do you need to choose?

  • A baseline file. The historical EPW for your site, from a source such as Climate.OneBuilding.org or the EnergyPlus weather library. Add the site's DDY file too if you want future design days.
  • A future year. FutureWeather offers 2035 to 2090. Each year stands for a 30-year window around it, so 2050 covers 2036–2065.
  • An emissions scenario. One of four Shared Socioeconomic Pathways used in CMIP6, from SSP1-2.6 (low emissions) through SSP2-4.5 (intermediate) and SSP3-7.0 (high) to SSP5-8.5 (very high) [4].
  • Climate models. The average of all 23 models is the default. Individual models show how much the models disagree.

Worked example: Seattle-Tacoma, 2050, SSP2-4.5

We took the free TMYx file for Seattle-Tacoma International Airport (USA_WA_Seattle-Tacoma.Intl.AP.727930_TMYx.2011-2025) and its DDY from Climate.OneBuilding.org, and generated a 2050 file under SSP2-4.5 using the average of all 23 models.

  • FutureWeather read the baseline period, 2011–2025, from the file's header.
  • The future window for 2050 is 2036–2065.
  • The job returned a future EPW, STAT and DDY, each named …_FUTURE_2050_ssp245.
MetricBaseline (2011–2025)2050, SSP2-4.5Change
Annual mean dry-bulb temperature12.1 °C13.3 °C+1.2 °C
Heating design dry-bulb (99.6%)−2.9 °C−1.2 °C+1.7 °C
Cooling design dry-bulb (0.4%)30.4 °C31.0 °C+0.6 °C
Heating degree-days (18 °C base)2,3402,025−315 (−13%)
Cooling degree-days (18 °C base)193304+111 (+58%)

Annual means are from the hourly EPW data and design temperatures from the DDY files. Degree-days are calculated the same way for both files, from daily mean temperatures; for the baseline this reproduces the values in the source STAT file. The future files were generated on 18 November 2026 with generator version 1.0.0.

For Seattle, the cold extreme warms more than the hot one: the 99.6% heating design temperature rises by 1.7 °C, but the 0.4% cooling design temperature by only 0.6 °C. Cooling degree-days, meanwhile, rise by more than half from a low base. Peak sizing conditions and annual heating and cooling demand can change by very different amounts, so it's worth checking both.

One thing to watch: the DESIGN CONDITIONS line in the header of a morphed EPW still holds the historical values. Take future design conditions from the future DDY instead.
