How are future weather files made? Morphing explained

By Rowan Schmidt, Head of Product, FutureWeather · November 18, 2026

Most future weather files for building simulation are made by morphing. You start from a real historical weather file and adjust every hour by the change that climate models project between a baseline period and a future period. Temperatures are shifted and stretched, quantities such as wind speed and precipitation are scaled, and the original file's hour-by-hour sequence of weather is kept. FutureWeather does this with 23 CMIP6 climate models, building on the open-source Future Weather Generator method.

The idea behind morphing

Belcher, Hacker and Powell set out morphing for building design weather in 2005 [1]. Rather than generating weather from scratch, it takes an observed weather file and applies the climate change signal from climate models to each hour, using three basic operations:

  • Shift: add the projected change to every value (used for air pressure, for example).
  • Stretch: multiply every value by a projected ratio (used for wind speed and precipitation).
  • Shift and stretch: move the average and widen or narrow the spread around it (used for dry-bulb temperature, where daily highs and lows can change by different amounts).

The same approach underpins tools such as CCWeatherGen [2] and the open-source Future Weather Generator, whose peer-reviewed method FutureWeather builds on [3]. If you're new to weather files, start with what a future weather file is and when you need one.

Step 1: Start with a real weather file

Everything begins with a historical EPW for the site: 8,760 hourly records of temperature, humidity, pressure, solar radiation, wind, sky cover and precipitation. FutureWeather reads the site's latitude and longitude from the file's LOCATION line. If the file covers a leap year, the 29 February rows are removed so that every file has 8,760 hours. You can also upload the site's DDY design-day file if you want future design days.

Step 2: Fix the baseline and future periods

Morphing measures the change between two climates: the baseline that the historical file represents, and the future window you are targeting.

  • Future window. Each target year stands for 30 years, from 14 years before it to 15 years after. A 2050 file represents 2036–2065. The FutureWeather app offers target years from 2035 to 2090.
  • Baseline. FutureWeather matches the baseline to the file's own period of record. It reads that from the file header (for example Period of Record=2011-2025 in TMYx files) or, failing that, from the file name. Periods shorter than 15 years are widened to 15 years around their midpoint, and files that state no period use 1985–2014. You can also pin a baseline yourself, for example with the Grasshopper plugin's Base input. Every job records the baseline it used and how it was chosen.

The baseline matters because a mismatch counts part of the warming twice, or misses it. Across eight US cities, a fixed 1985–2014 baseline over-corrected TMYx 2011–2025 files by about 0.7 °C on average, roughly 38% of the projected 2050 change under SSP2-4.5, and under-corrected older TMY3 files by about 0.3 °C. That is why FutureWeather moved to matched baselines in September 2026 (see platform enhancements).

One practical detail: CMIP6 historical simulations end in 2014 [4]. For baselines that run past 2014, each model's historical run is joined to that model's run of the chosen scenario from 2015 onwards.

Step 3: Get the climate change signal from the models

The changes come from CMIP6, the latest coordinated set of global climate model experiments [4], run under the Shared Socioeconomic Pathway scenarios SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5, SSP3-7.0 and SSP5-8.5 [5].

  • For each model, FutureWeather takes monthly values at the site, combining the four nearest grid cells weighted by distance, and averages each calendar month over the baseline and the future window.
  • With the default ensemble of 23 models, those monthly averages are averaged across the models first, and one change per calendar month is taken from the result.
  • The change is a difference for mean, daily maximum and daily minimum temperature, specific humidity, sea-level pressure, cloud cover, and shortwave and longwave radiation. It is a ratio for precipitation, wind speed and snow depth.

If you pick specific models instead, the same steps use only those models. Parametric runs go further and produce one file per individual model, so you can see the spread between them.

For dry-bulb temperature, each hour's value T becomes T + ΔT + α(T − T̄). Here T̄ is that month's mean in the original file and ΔT is the projected change in mean temperature. The stretch factor α is the projected change in the daily temperature range (the change in daily maximum minus the change in daily minimum) divided by the file's mean daily range. When daily highs are projected to warm more than daily lows, α is positive and warm hours move further than cool ones.

Step 5: Let the extremes respond

Standard morphing adjusts every hour using rules built from monthly averages. The hottest and coldest hours, however, don't necessarily change by the same amount as the average, and extremes are often what drive peak loads and overheating. FutureWeather adds a step after the temperature morph. It reshapes each month's distribution of hourly temperatures so that the warming at each percentile follows the pattern in 3-hourly temperature data from one CMIP6 model, GFDL-ESM4. The monthly mean is left unchanged, and the adjustment to any single hour is capped at 5 °C. This step is on by default for every job (see platform enhancements).

Step 6: Write the output files

  • EPW. The year field is set to the target year. The ground temperatures and the typical and extreme periods in the header are recalculated from the new data. The COMMENTS lines record the generator version, models, scenario, and the baseline and future periods. The DESIGN CONDITIONS line is carried over unchanged, so it still describes the baseline climate.
  • STAT. Always produced from the future EPW, including heating and cooling degree-days at 10 °C and 18 °C bases.
  • DDY. If you upload the site's historical DDY, FutureWeather calculates design statistics (heating 99.6% and 99%, cooling 0.4%, 1% and 2%) from both the historical and the future EPW. It then applies the difference to the design values in your DDY, so the design days move consistently with the morphed EPW.

Worked example: Seattle-Tacoma in July

For the Seattle-Tacoma TMYx file (baseline 2011–2025, read from its header) morphed to 2050 under SSP2-4.5 with the 23-model average (future window 2036–2065), here is what happened to July's hourly dry-bulb temperatures:

July dry-bulb temperatureBaseline file2050, SSP2-4.5Change
Monthly mean20.0 °C21.2 °C+1.2 °C
Mean daily range11.4 °C11.1 °C−0.3 °C
Hottest hour34.4 °C35.0 °C+0.6 °C
Coolest hour10.6 °C12.5 °C+1.9 °C

In Seattle's July, the cool hours warm more than the warm ones: the coolest hour rises by 1.9 °C, the monthly mean by 1.2 °C and the hottest hour by only 0.6 °C, so the average daily range narrows slightly. That is the shift-and-stretch and extremes steps at work: the change is not one number added to every hour.

The hottest and coolest hours also fall at exactly the same times in both files, in the afternoon of 30 July and early on 4 July. Morphing keeps the baseline's weather sequence and changes how warm each hour is, not when the warm and cool spells happen.

Values are from the hourly dry-bulb data in the baseline EPW and in the future EPW generated on 18 November 2026 with generator version 1.0.0.

What morphing can and can't do

  • It keeps the original weather sequence. Storms, heatwaves and cloudy spells happen on the same days as in the baseline file, just shifted or scaled. Morphing doesn't create new kinds of events.
  • Changes are applied month by month. Each calendar month gets one set of changes. That captures seasonal differences, but not changes in how weather varies within a month, apart from the temperature extremes step.
  • The result is only as good as the baseline. Start from a recent, good-quality file for a station near your site.
  • An ensemble average smooths over disagreement between models. To see the range of possible outcomes, generate files for several scenarios or for individual models.

How we tested it

As we built FutureWeather, we compared its core morph with the original Future Weather Generator across 184 combinations of climate model, scenario and year. Results for temperature, humidity, pressure, solar radiation and wind were typically within 1% of the original. Every file also records the generator version that produced it in its COMMENTS line, so you can always trace which version of the method made it.

Try it on your own project

Generate a future EPW, STAT and optional DDY for your site in the FutureWeather web app (new accounts get 5 free credits), or script it with the Python package or the API. The full list of method changes is on the platform enhancements page.
